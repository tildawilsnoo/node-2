# Narrative Subplots in the Boston Marathon Bombing Wikipedia Editing History

*Analysis of 1,655 revisions across the Investigation, Suspects, Other Arrests and Detentions, and Conflicting Reports sections — April 15 to May 14, 2013.*

---

## 1. The Saudi Suspect False Report

Within hours of the bombings, the New York Post reported that police had arrested a Saudi Arabian national as a suspect. The claim enters the Wikipedia article almost immediately, and what follows is one of the sharpest early edit wars in the dataset: editors add the report, others remove it citing reliability concerns, and it gets re-added and re-removed in rapid succession across April 15–16. The tension is never really about whether the Post story is true — most editors seem to sense it isn't — but about whether a disputed claim from a live news cycle belongs in an encyclopedia at all. It ultimately finds its resting place not as a fact but as an exhibit: moved to the "Conflicting reports" section, reframed as a documented instance of media failure. The arc from "reported fact" to "cautionary example" happens in under 48 hours.

---

## 2. Suspect Identification — Unknown to Named

On April 18, the FBI released surveillance photos of two unidentified men carrying backpacks. The Wikipedia article at that point can only say "two suspects." Within 24 hours of the press conference, the names Tamerlan and Dzhokhar Tsarnaev appear for the first time — initially misspelled, with uncertain ages and almost no biographical detail. What follows over the next several days is a dense accumulation: spellings corrected, ages verified, the brothers' Chechen origin established, their years in the United States pinned down, their social media profiles surfaced, and the narrative of the FBI's photographic evidence carefully constructed around eyewitness corroboration. Peak editing activity — 309 edits — falls on April 20, the day Dzhokhar is captured, when editors race to integrate the confirmation that the suspects in the photos were indeed the perpetrators.

---

## 3. Jeff Bauman — Witness to Icon

Jeff Bauman first appears in the article on April 20 as a single awkward sentence — "Jeff Bauman, who lost both legs, claimed he providing detailed descriptions" — a typo-ridden placeholder that barely registers. Over the following days, editors return to him repeatedly, each pass adding specificity: his location relative to the second bomb, the fact that he regained consciousness in the hospital and immediately asked for pen and paper, and finally his written note to the FBI — "bag, saw the guy, looked right at me." By the time the text stabilizes, Bauman has been transformed from an unnamed victim into the article's most human moment: a man who lost his legs and whose first act upon waking was to help catch the people who did it. The editing of his story is a small case study in how Wikipedia constructs narrative significance through incremental detail.

---

## 4. Sunil Tripathi — Documenting a False Accusation

Sunil Tripathi, a Brown University student who had been missing since March, was falsely identified as a bombing suspect by users on Reddit and 4chan on the night of April 18–19. He enters the Wikipedia article on April 26 with blunt, unmediated language — "wrongly identified brown Indian American student who suffered from depression" — framing that reads more like a Reddit post than an encyclopedia entry. Editors gradually sand down the language and, crucially, shift the frame: the story stops being about Tripathi's identity and becomes about the behavior of the platforms that accused him. He is moved to the "Conflicting reports" section alongside the Saudi suspect story, and the prose is rewritten to center Reddit and social media as the subjects. Tripathi's body had been found in the Providence River on April 25, one day before he first appeared in the article — a detail editors handle carefully, with visible discomfort about how to contextualize it.

---

## 5. Building the Radicalization Narrative

The question of why the Tsarnaev brothers bombed the marathon is assembled slowly and strictly by citation. It does not arrive as a single claim but as a sequence of sourced additions spread across nearly a month: Dzhokhar's VKontakte profile lists his worldview as "Islam" and contains links to Chechen separatist content (April 19); Tamerlan is described as having developed a "growing Salafist and radical viewpoint" (April 21); the Russian FSB is reported to have warned the FBI about Tamerlan years earlier (April 21); the bomb-making instructions are traced to the Al-Qaeda-affiliated magazine *Inspire* (April 26); and finally, Dzhokhar's own interrogation statement — that the brothers acted to defend Islam against U.S. wars in Iraq and Afghanistan — enters the article in May. No editor synthesizes these into a single causal claim. The ideology narrative is always presented through attribution, never assertion, which is itself a kind of editorial argument about what Wikipedia can and cannot say during an active investigation.

---

## 6. "Conflicting Reports" — Meta-Documentation

The "Conflicting reports" section does not exist at the start of the dataset. It has to be invented. Editors first create a section called "Errors in reporting," which proves immediately contentious — on May 2, one editor renames it "Conflicting reports," another reverts it within minutes, and a third intervenes to argue that the original name misdirects blame: journalists often accurately reported incorrect information they were given, so the errors were in the information, not the reporting. "Conflicting reports" wins. What the section ultimately contains is a curated record of the information environment surrounding the bombings: the Saudi suspect story, the Reddit misidentifications, the JFK Library fire confusion. It is Wikipedia editing at its most self-aware — a community deciding not just what happened, but how to document the fact that so much of what was initially reported did not.
